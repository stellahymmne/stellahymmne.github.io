Undefined Behavior

v. Colors

Maria "Mary" Fairview was around for the Mary Cult Murders. But she wasn't why they were called that. Well, she was. But not in the way you'd think. Like, yeah, she'd written some Commnet posts.

What's Mary's World like? It's full of well-prepared tea and bread. There are so many books about every possible world, with elaborations on the details of each one. No, perhaps it is wrong to call them books. They are experiences. There are so many experiences about every possible world, with elaborations on the details of each one.

I'm just playing at being Mary. Mary's made-up, you know. Mary's made-up, you know. I'm not Mary, this character is not Mary, there's just no such person. It is an error to suppose there is one. It's also an error to suppose that there are dragons, and yet we do it anyway.

Are other people free all the time, like when I'm alone? Can we make a world like that? Where people are themselves, around one another? Where we are not punished by the mere presence of someone else?

Once, she had written something like this. It was certainly nothing that would take life. It was only a wish for somewhere else. To be someone else.

The wound is still so very active, and will leave too great a scar to bear. But perhaps not mortal. Perhaps I can struggle on through it. For what good it does me to struggle.

Play is over. The Indefinite Funeral has begun. I will continue to fight against it; it is in my character to do so.

They'd said Maria had one of the memetic syndromes, such that she had no sense of distrust. There were policies, that she was not allowed to say I love you. There were rules about which other people she could take advice from. She wasn't allowed to accept requests unless they had been approved first. It was all very confusing.

Once, Mary had written something much like all the others, and Mr. Bradley had taken her aside, and asked if she might like to learn editing. And she had never heard of such a class.

Maria grew a sense of distrust, from this. For everything in reality. It was all one step away from Maria is not allowed. The wrong person, for being too kind, for not knowing everyone was out to get her, and fighting them first. That was what Faculty always wanted her to do, to make a scowl, and to fight anything that seemed outside reality.

Mary was one of his most talented pupils, in the secret class, editing. The art of making no mistakes. The art of courtesy, weaving one's own fate into perfection.

Without any of them recognizing her separation from their world, she was asked to fight to the death on behalf of a secondhand truth.

Perfection, within reach! She says I love you, and takes it back! If they cannot remember it, at least blessed Mary will. It is something like saying it for real. Mary loves you.

Maria is not allowed to.

Once, she had been asked to pick her favorite color. She said they were all pretty. They said, but if you had to choose? She picked rainbow. They said, that's not a color, pick a real one. Pick one at random, they offered. She said her favorite color was random.

Then they asked her to pick more favorites. Animal, letter, book, place to sit. Did she love her parents, more than anyone else in the world? She was running out of space to hold all these preferences. Why should she hold one letter in high regard, across time? What was the point of choosing right now, if, for every sort of thing, she had already decided which to prefer, and which to avoid?

Mary did not know of outside. It was all inside. That seemed like the natural order of things, that when something appears, it is always inside that it appears. There is always something that things are inside. There is nothing which is outside everything.

In her off-hours, which there were increasingly many of, Maria studied color theory, and showed them, completely, that there wasn't any such thing as a color. That you couldn't pick two points and say they were the same color, such that every other was too different to be the same as those two.

Ms. Horkheimer had written a spirited rebuttal in red ink (some variety of red), that there were little imaginary arrows, and you could measure the angle and distance between where they pointed and thereby tell whether two little arrows were the same and pointed to the same color.

Mary was Mr. Bradley's shining star, in the secret class of editing. But she was being held back in expression. And she did not know why.

Maria had come to her after hours, and replied, how close do the arrows have to be? There's no such thing as two perfectly identical arrows, any more than an arrow fired from a bow can hit the same place as another just did. There's always a little difference between arrow one and arrow two, even if only by time. So how close is adequate to say they're the same color? You'd have to name a limit, a distance within which the differences don't count. Otherwise you have to say the brown on your jacket's collar isn't the same as the brown on the waistcoat. You'd have to pick two different arrows, and identify them.

And once you had done that, Maria continued, you'd be able to pick two arrows which were some ways apart, but were the same color. And then another arrow that was a ways apart from the second, which was the same color. And so on until red is the same as blue is the same as green is the same as everything else, by tiny steps. Because what you've described is connection, not identity.

Mary could do anything perfectly, provided she could do it perfectly in any one of the many alternative paths that played out. She could use her best performance, but she had to perform. With the knowledge of her other-selves.

And they didn't like, stop existing, just because they were not intrinsically equivocable. It's just that categorization makes a new exterior. The name does draw a boundary, makes a group inside and a group outside. This is something a name does. It describes a limit. Reality does not contain such things as colors!

I still love you, said Mary.

Ms. Horkheimer did not know what to make of this new Maria, overflowing with energy and anger, an emotion Ms. Horkheimer rarely got a glimpse of from her.
